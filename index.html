Every project, and how far behind it is

Every contract the Board has reviewed, oldest approval first. Open a row for approval history, terms and sources.

Developers report commercial operation dates, and those dates move. This model takes the slippage this portfolio has already recorded and projects it forward, so the question is not when each developer says a project will arrive but when it arrives if the next amendment looks like the last one.

Scenario

How to read the numbers

  • Capacity and availability both come from LUMA's Daily Availability Report of October 24, 2026: 115 units at 33 plants, 6,460 MW of nameplate and 3,092 MW available. The same figures drive the headline tiles, the map and the municipality panel, so the page carries one capacity total throughout. EIA-860M supplies only the coordinates that place each plant, and each municipality is derived by testing those coordinates against the Census boundary file rather than read off a plant name.
  • Nameplate is what a unit is rated at; available is what the operator reported it could actually deliver that day. The two differ by 3,369 MW across the fleet, and 2,854 MW of that sits in 67 units that delivered nothing at all.
  • Fuel is assigned by unit name, because the availability report does not state it. San Juan 5 and 6, the TM units, Costa Sur 5 and 6 and EcoEléctrica are treated as gas, AES as coal, the San Juan, Palo Seco and Aguirre steam units as heavy fuel oil, and every peaking and combined-cycle turbine as distillate. EIA codes the San Juan and Aguirre combined cycles as distillate instead, which would move about 1,070 MW from gas to oil; this is the least settled figure on the page.
  • Commercial operation dates resolve through one waterfall: the Appendix A amendment first, then the P3A executive briefing of October 31, 2026, then LUMA's October 2026 collaborative report, then the original COD. Every cell names the document that supplied the date it shows. Quarters are taken at the quarter end.
  • Days since approval counts from the first Board approval, with the position against a base case of commercial operation 24 months after approval underneath it.
  • Project capacity is the contracted amount in the most recent Board letter or regulatory record. Storage is shown in MW of power, with MWh where disclosed, and is always priced as a capacity rate in dollars per MW-month so the batteries can be compared with one another.
  • Energy prices are shown to two decimal places. Several contracts round to the same figure as a result, including the AES Salinas and Jobos solar rates, which both read $0.10/kWh against $0.1042 and $0.1038 in the tracker.

What is and isn't included

  • Included on the grid side: every generating unit the system operator dispatches — PREPA's plants run by Genera PR, the hydro PREPA retains, the coal and gas independents, the FEMA-funded temporary units at San Juan and Palo Seco, and the utility-scale renewables under contract.
  • The 1 MW Bechara wind unit appears in the availability report with no corresponding EIA record, so it has no coordinates and is not drawn on the map.
  • Excluded because the system operator does not dispatch them: roughly 1,230 MW of rooftop solar, behind-the-meter industrial cogeneration, and customer-sited generators.
  • The approved pipeline counts contracts the Oversight Board has approved since 2021. Capacity still before the Board is listed in the table and drawn on the map with a dashed outline, but excluded from every approved total, including the headline figures.
  • Coquí Power's eleven sites appear as 22 rows, one per contract: the eleven solar PPOAs were approved in July 2026 while the eleven companion storage contracts remain under Board review.
  • Genera's six batteries and four peaker sites are PREPA-owned programmes rather than power purchase agreements, so they carry no contracted rate. Only the Costa Sur peakers are approved, in January 2025; the other three sites reached the Board in October 2026 and are counted as under review.
  • The Board's November 28, 2025 conditional approval of the Genera–NFE multisite gas supply agreement is a fuel contract, so it is noted here rather than mapped.
  • Genera PR's parent, New Fortress Energy, closed a restructuring on September 11, 2026 that shifts control to creditors; operator labels reflect the contract, not that change.

Where the sources disagree, and what is shown

What the availability report shows

  • Parsed from the Data tab of LUMA's Daily Availability Report for October 24, 2026: 115 units, 6,460.1 MW of nameplate and 3,091.5 MW available, or 47.9%. Every block subtotal reconciles exactly to the subtotals printed on the sheet. EcoEléctrica and AES are taken from the gross block, not the net one.
  • 2,853.6 MW across 67 units carried nameplate and delivered nothing that day, including Aguirre 1 (450 MW), Costa Sur 5 (410 MW), all four San Juan steam units (400 MW) and both Palo Seco 1 and 2 (170 MW).
  • Against a forecast peak of 2,727 MW the system held 111.6 MW of reserves against 675 MW required. The report puts its own availability rate at 45.7%, which excludes solar, wind and landfill gas from supply.
  • Fuel is assigned by unit name, since the report does not state it: San Juan 5 and 6 and the TM units are treated as gas on the basis of the New Fortress LNG supply, Costa Sur 5 and 6 as gas, EcoEléctrica as gas, AES as coal, the San Juan, Palo Seco and Aguirre steam units as heavy fuel oil, and all peaking and combined-cycle turbines as distillate. That puts the fleet at 53% oil and 34% gas. EIA codes the San Juan and Aguirre combined cycles as distillate instead, which would move about 1,070 MW from gas to oil. This remains the least settled figure on the page.

The existing fleet, rebuilt from the primary filing

  • The asset list previously rested on the WRI Global Power Plant Database and on EIA figures taken from a third-party summary. It is now built directly from EIA-860M, September 2026: 105 generators across 38 plants in the Electric Utility and independent power producer sectors, 5,704.0 MW of nameplate and 5,167.4 MW of net summer capacity, plus the two landfill-gas sites and the 350 MW of FEMA temporary units EIA does not carry. Total 6,058.8 MW nameplate, 5,522.2 MW net summer.
  • This resolved all four discrepancies the old list flagged against its own sources. Costa Sur is 1,032 MW, not 862: EIA still carries units 3 and 4, commissioned in 1962 and 1963. Palo Seco is 728 MW, not 582, for the same reason. EcoEléctrica, at 580 MW, is now covered by the plant's own EIA record.
  • CIRO One Salinas is no longer counted as existing capacity. It is in testing, not commercial operation, and EIA does not list it as operating. That removes a roughly 140 MW double-count with the approved pipeline.
  • Palo Seco unit 4 carries 216 MW of nameplate and zero net summer capacity in EIA's record, against LUMA's report of 180 MW of actual output in September 2026. Both figures are shown; the discrepancy is unresolved.
  • EIA's planned inventory for Puerto Rico contains 22.2 MW. None of the Board-approved pipeline has reached the stage at which EIA records a generator as planned.

What the rebuild changed, and one unresolved conflict

The fuel mix moved more than the total did. EIA codes each generator by its reported primary energy source, and on that basis the fleet is far more oil-bound than the previous list showed.

The unresolved conflict: EIA codes San Juan's 2008 combined-cycle units and Aguirre's combined cycle as distillate fuel oil, roughly 1,070 MW that the previous list showed as natural gas. San Juan 5 and 6 are documented as burning LNG supplied by New Fortress Energy, so EIA's coding may simply lag the conversion. EIA's codes are shown because it is the primary source, but the gas share is probably understated and the oil share overstated by several hundred megawatts. Treat the fuel split as the weakest part of this rebuild.

  • The AES energy storage agreements were amended to raise capacity by 10 MW to 110 MW at Jobos and by 75 MW to 175 MW at Salinas, with no change to the 25-year terms or the maximum payable amounts. Both batteries are priced at $23,696/MW-month with a 2% escalator, which replaces the figure previously derived from the contract ceilings.
  • Rates and a site supplied by the project owner also closed four gaps: the Guayama Solar Energy battery at $25,500/MW-month through year five and $21,250 thereafter, San Juan Solar at $0.145/kWh, Xzerta Energy Volt in Hatillo, and Xzerta-Tec Solar I's March 2021 approval confirmed against the Board's own record rather than inferred.
  • The commercial operation dates now come from two columns of Appendix A and one deck. Original COD and Amended & Approved COD are taken straight from the tracker; where the amended date matches the original, or none was issued, the column reads “Not applicable”. Beneath it sits the date in the Executive Briefing to the Governor and the Board of October 31, 2026, which supplies a date for 30 projects and is treated as the operative one, replacing LUMA's Table 7 in that role.
  • The briefing gives commercial operation dates for Genera's six batteries and four peaker sites for the first time: Cambalache December 2026, Vega Baja March 2027, Costa Sur June 2027, Aguirre and Yabucoa September 2027 and Palo Seco June 2028 for the batteries; Costa Sur and Yabucoa March 2028 and Jobos and Daguao September 2028 for the peakers.   • Two conflicts the briefing introduces. It puts Energiza in operation in December 2030, against the October 30, 2030 confirmed earlier; the briefing date is carried here. And its own slides disagree on the peakers, the summary table giving Costa Sur March 2028 and Yabucoa March 2028 where the map on the next page shows June 2028 and September 2027; the summary table is used.
  • The briefing also lists the battery contracts' prices in cents per kilowatt-hour, but the figures match the dollars-per-MW-month rates almost exactly — .23¢ against $23,546, .26¢ against $26,900, .33¢ against $33,750. That is a unit slip in the deck, so the MW-month figures are kept. The AES storage agreements are the exception: their 24¢ genuinely is per kilowatt-hour.
  • The briefing puts Genera's batteries at 530 MW installed and 430 MW usable, and splits the usable capacity differently across the six sites than Appendix A splits the nameplate. The Appendix A figures are kept, since both sum to 430 MW and only Appendix A states a per-site nameplate.
  • Energy prices are shown to two decimal places, matching how the briefing quotes them in whole cents. Several contracts round to the same figure as a result: the AES Salinas and Jobos solar rates both read $0.10/kWh, against $0.1042 and $0.1038 in the tracker.
  • LUMA's Table 7 carries 18 projects, each matched here by row name, developer and capacity. Its commercial operation dates are superseded by Appendix A and the P3A deck, but its interconnection progress is kept and shown as a tag in the status column: 14 of the 18 report zero progress, one is complete, and the rest sit between 50 and 75 per cent.
  • Four of LUMA's rows disagree with the contract record: Salinas repeats the Jobos capacities, Vega Baja reads 49 MW against the Board's 60 MW, CIRO One tracks phase 1 only, and San Germán carries the original date. None of those now drives a figure on this page.
  • Every storage contract is shown as a capacity price in $/MW-month, so the batteries can be compared with one another. The AES Salinas and Jobos energy storage agreements are written per kWh of discharge in the tracker, at $0.2418 and $0.242/kWh; both are carried here at the confirmed $23,696/MW-month with a 2% escalator.
  • Two projects have no separate storage price to show at all: Coamo and YFN Yabucoa bundle the battery into a single blended energy rate with the solar. Genera's six batteries and the Guayama Solar Energy battery carry no rate, the first because they are not power purchase agreements and the second because none was supplied.
  • Rates for the submissions still under review are the figures supplied with the project list, not approved terms: Emerald II Helios $33,750/MW-month over six hours, Montalva storage $29,325/MW-month over five hours, Yabucoa Energy and Xzerta Energy Volt $15,500/MW-month, Montalva solar $0.1245/kWh and Coquí storage $23,500/MW-month. They are submitted terms, not approved ones.
  • Peaker capacities and the Costa Sur approval come from the project owner, not from LUMA's report, which gives only the 244 MW program total and no per-site split. Its milestone table also differs on timing, showing Costa Sur at Q3 2027 and Jobos and Daguao as pending decommissioning authorisation, against the owner's Q4 2027 to Q1 2028, Q2 2028 and Q2 to Q3 2028.
  • Gothams carries a fuel-indexed rate rather than a fixed one: $0.188/kWh tied to the Title Transfer Facility index, so it is not comparable with the fixed prices elsewhere in the table.
  • Two environmental inspections required in the next six months — Costa Sur by November 5, 2026 and Palo Seco by April 2027 — are reported as unfunded in Genera's approved budget, as is the Aguirre 2 overhaul that would restore 115 MW.
  • The Board flagged the Coquí pricing itself: eight of the eleven projects exceed the ~$157/MWh target portfolio weighted average LCOE, the highest reaching about $200/MWh, and the highest-priced contract is roughly 29% above the highest-priced Tranche 1 solar project. Approval rests on PREPA and the Energy Bureau enforcing the portfolio pricing mechanism.
  • Interconnection studies were not complete when either accelerated-evaluation contract was approved. LUMA told the Board it cannot certify available interconnection capacity until they are, which puts the ITC deadlines of July 4, 2026 for construction start and December 31, 2027 for placed-in-service at risk.
  • Unlike CK1, the Coquí contracts have no downward rate adjustment if actual PREPA interconnection costs fall below $20 million; the providers' estimated costs are baked into the base rates instead.
  • Two expected CODs have passed without commercial operation: CS/UR Juncos (Sep 2025) and CFE Jobos (May 2026). YFN Yabucoa's lapsed August 2026 date is superseded by LUMA's December 2026.
  • LUMA reports the Convergent storage projects at Peñuelas, Ponce and Caguas, and the Tetris solar project, are in the process of being acquired by Pattern.
  • Two separate contracts carry the Yabucoa Energy Park name and both are counted: the Board-approved 80 MW Go Green battery from Tranche 1, and the 100 MW Yabucoa Energy battery submitted under the accelerated evaluation procurement and still under review. The 100 MW of storage in LUMA's Table 7 is the latter.
